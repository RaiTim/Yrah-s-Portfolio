Reflection

Stepping from Field Study 1’s broad school observation into Field Study 2’s focused instructional analysis transformed how I view the craft of teaching. Observing five distinct Grade 8 sections taught me that a lesson plan is a living blueprint, not a rigid script. Even when teaching the exact same learning competency, an effective teacher constantly reads the room—adjusting questioning depth for SPJ and Robotics learners, strengthening visual scaffolds for SPTVE and regular sections, and injecting brisk interactive energy when the clock strikes 6:00 PM. Most importantly, I realized that assessment and instructional materials work best when they serve as bridges rather than barriers. Simple, purposeful tools—like a well-structured graphic organizer, a relatable local example, or a five-minute vocabulary warm-up—can turn an intimidating reading passage into an “aha!” moment for struggling learners. As I step further into teaching assistance and demonstration responsibilities in Field Study 2, I carry a clear conviction: great teaching lies in thoughtful preparation paired with the empathy and agility to meet every class right where they are.

Summary of CBAR Methodology (Chapter 2)

This study employs a quantitative pre-experimental one-group pre-test–post-test design (O₁ → X → O₂) across a four-week instructional cycle to evaluate classroom-level reading gains within an intact section (Fraenkel et al., 2012). Using total population sampling—specifically purposive intact-group selection (Andrade, 2021)—the participants consist of all N = 34 learners in Grade 8 – Jaena at Gordon Heights National High School, where baseline Phil-IRI results showed 100% word-reading accuracy but 0% Grade 8 comprehension readiness, with 58.82% falling into Instructional and Frustration levels.

During Week 1, following written parental consent and learner assent, the researcher administers a 30-item multiple-choice Pre-Test (O_1) aligned with a Table of Specifications (TOS), comprising 15 context-clue vocabulary items and 15 literal and inferential comprehension items, after content validation by three English or reading specialists. Across Weeks 2 and 3, learners complete six print-based CLUE-QUEST Detective Case File worksheets (three per week, 30–40 minutes per session): Case Files #1–#3 scaffold explicit definition, synonym, and antonym clues, while Case Files #4–#6 advance to inference clues and multi-paragraph passages, paired with printed Case Logs where learners record Experience Points (XP) to progress from Junior Detective (0–149 XP) to Field Detective (150–299 XP) and Master Detective (300+ XP). Using a print-only format maintains equitable participation and learning continuity regardless of device or internet access or heat-related Modular Distance Learning shifts.

In Week 4, an equivalent TOS-aligned 30-item Post-Test (O_2) is administered to measure learning gains. Descriptive statistics—mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage—summarize pre-test and post-test performance, while a paired-samples t-test at α = 0.05 tests whether mean score differences are statistically significant (with the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test designated as a non-parametric fallback if normality assumptions are violated). All procedures uphold Bell and Bryman’s (2007) ethical principles and the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173) through anonymity via learner codes or detective codenames, secure data storage, voluntary participation, non-maleficence, data integrity, and transparent disclosure of responsible AI-assisted academic editing.
