Field Study 1 · FS1–1

School Environment and Communication Process

School Profile

Gordon Heights National High School (GHNHS), is a public secondary educational institution located along Keith Street, Barangay Gordon Heights, Olongapo City, Zambales. Established in 1979 and originally known as Gordon Heights Barangay High School, the institution has continuously provided accessible and quality educational opportunities to learners in the community for over four decades. Operating under the Department of Education – Schools Division Office of Olongapo City, it offers the Basic Education Program alongside diverse special curricular offerings—including the Special Program in Science, Technology, Engineering, Mathematics and Robotics (STEM-R), Special Program in Information and Communication Technology (ICT), Special Program in the Arts (SPA), Special Program in Sports (SPS), Special Program in Foreign Language (SPFL), Open High School Program (OHSP), and Special Program in Technical-Vocational Education (SPTVE)—that cater to students’ varied interests, talents, and learning needs.

Guided by its school mantra, “Empowering minds and character to foster a culture of excellence,” and anchored in its core values of I-CARE (“I am compassionate, accommodating, resilient, and excellent”), GHNHS aligns with the MATATAG agenda to provide a supportive, inclusive learning environment that promotes academic excellence, holistic development, and responsible citizenship.

School Environment

1. Library

The school library serves as a quiet, resource-rich hub that supports independent research, reading literacy, and academic enrichment for both students and teachers. Equipped with textbooks, reference materials, and study spaces, it fosters a culture of continuous learning and intellectual curiosity outside the regular classroom.

2. Care Center (Guidance and Counseling Room)

The Care Center serves as a safe, confidential space dedicated to supporting the emotional, psychological, and behavioral well-being of learners. Through one-on-one counseling, career guidance, and student wellness programs, it ensures that students receive compassionate mentorship and intervention whenever they face personal or academic challenges.

3. School Garden

The school garden functions as an outdoor learning space that promotes environmental stewardship, sustainability, and practical agricultural skills among students. Beyond beautifying the campus, it provides hands-on opportunities for learners to connect science and TLE concepts with real-world care for nature.

4. Science Laboratory

The Science Laboratory provides a specialized, hands-on environment where students can conduct experiments, test hypotheses, and observe scientific phenomena firsthand. Equipped with laboratory apparatus and safety tools, it bridges theoretical lessons with practical inquiry and critical thinking.

5. Speech Laboratory

Designed with individual acoustic cubicles and headsets, the Speech Laboratory provides an interactive environment for developing students’ oral communication, pronunciation, and listening comprehension skills. This specialized setup minimizes distractions and allows learners to practice language fluency, public speaking, and audio-assisted exercises with confidence.

6. English Nook

English Nook serves as a collaborative workspace where us student teachers prepare lesson plans, check student outputs, and exchange instructional ideas. During our vacant periods in the observation phase, this welcoming corner became our home base for reflection, mentorship discussions, and quiet preparation before stepping into the classroom.

7. Music Room

The Music Room serves as a creative, expressive space where students explore musical theory, vocal training, and instrumental performance. Equipped with musical instruments and rehearsal areas, it nurtures artistic talent, cultural appreciation, and collaborative performance— particularly supporting learners in the school’s Special Program in the Arts (SPA).

8. School Canteen

The School Canteen provides a clean, accessible dining area where students, teachers, and staff can purchase nutritious, affordable meals and snacks throughout the school day. Beyond promoting proper health and wellness to keep learners energized for their classes, it serves as a welcoming social hub where students interact and build camaraderie during breaks.

Communication Process in the School

Effective communication is the backbone of daily operations and instructional success at Gordon Heights National High School (GHNHS). Throughout my internship, I observed how clear, timely, and respectful communication keeps administrators, faculty, staff, learners, and parents aligned toward shared educational goals.

Administrative-to-Faculty Communication (Top-Down): School announcements, DepEd memoranda, policy updates, and event schedules are systematically disseminated from the School Principal and Department Heads to the teaching staff through official memoranda, faculty meetings, Learning Action Cell (LAC) sessions, and designated online group chats. This ensures that every teacher is well-informed and prepared for school-wide initiatives.

Peer-to-Peer and Mentorship Communication (Horizontal): Within the faculty—particularly in our English Department—communication is collaborative and supportive. Teachers regularly share instructional resources, discuss student progress, and coordinate assessments. As a student teacher, I experienced this firsthand through daily pre- and post-observation conferences with my Cooperating Teacher, where constructive feedback and open dialogue guided my instructional growth.

Teacher-to-Student Communication: Inside the classroom, communication goes beyond delivering lesson content. Teachers establish clear classroom routines, learning objectives, and behavioral expectations while encouraging active student participation, questions, and two-way dialogue. Outside class hours, announcements regarding tasks, schedules, and reminders are reinforced through class officers and digital platforms.

School-to-Parent and Community Communication (Home-School Partnership): GHNHS maintains strong ties with families and the local community through General and Homeroom Parent-Teacher Association (PTA) assemblies, quarterly card-giving consultations, parent-teacher conferences, and official social media pages. When learners encounter academic or behavioral concerns, teachers and advisers communicate directly with parents to build supportive, unified interventions.

Overall, witnessing the school’s communication flow taught me that a thriving school community relies on transparency, active listening, and mutual respect across all levels.

Field Study 1 · FS1–2

Learner Characteristics

Interviewees’ photos were not permitted, and the learners are identified by code.

Learner A · Grade 8 – Asimov

The Energetic Socializer

During our brief check-in, Learner A enthusiastically shared that English class is most enjoyable when it involves team games, friendly competitions, or acting out scenarios with classmates. He admitted that sitting still through long slide presentations or silent reading tasks makes him restless and easily distracted by his friends. When asked what helps him focus, he noted that having a specific role in a group —such as being the team leader or presenter—keeps him motivated and accountable.

Learner B · Grade 8 – Jaena

The Quiet Perfectionist

Learner B expressed in a soft-spoken, thoughtful manner that she genuinely enjoys reading stories and writing essays where she has enough time to organize her thoughts carefully. However, she shared that being called on unexpectedly during whole-class recitation makes her anxious because she is afraid of mispronouncing words or giving an incomplete answer in front of everyone. She mentioned feeling much more confident when she is allowed to discuss her ideas with a seatmate first through Think-Pair-Share before speaking to the class.

Learner C · Grade 8 – Talaghay

The Visual & Creative Student

When asked about his learning preferences, Learner C explained that he understands lessons much faster when the teacher uses pictures, video clips, diagrams, or color-coded board work rather than purely verbal explanations. He shared that doodling in his notebook actually helps him concentrate while listening, though he sometimes worries teachers might think he is not paying attention. He expressed excitement whenever performance tasks allow him to draw, design posters, or create visual storyboards to show what he learned.

Learner D · Grade 8 – Zara

The Reluctant Reader

In a relaxed, one-on-one conversation, Learner D candidly admitted that long English paragraphs and unfamiliar vocabulary often make her feel overwhelmed, causing her to shut down or avoid participating. She shared that he learns best when complex English concepts are explained using simple, relatable Filipino or local examples, and when instructions are broken down step-by-step. She also noted that working alongside a patient peer buddy makes her feel less embarrassed to ask questions when she gets stuck.

Narrative Report and Reflection

Matching Learner Characteristics with Learning Activities

Observing and conversing with four distinct learners within the same Grade 8 class at Gordon Heights National High School dismantled the illusion that teaching the same grade level means teaching a uniform group of students. Even though Learners A, B, C, and D share the same age, curriculum, and classroom walls, each of them experiences the exact same lesson through a completely different cognitive and emotional lens.

When instructional activities intentionally align with learner characteristics, potential classroom struggles transform into strengths. For instance, assigning mixed-ability collaborative tasks allowed Learner A’s social energy, Learner B’s writing precision, Learner C’s visual creativity, and Learner D’s practical insights to complement one another rather than clash. Similarly, pairing graphic organizers and multimedia anchors with chunked instructions kept Learner C engaged while lowering Learner D’s anxiety around English texts. Meanwhile, incorporating structured Think-Pair-Share routines gave Learner B a safe, low-pressure bridge to share her brilliant ideas without the fear of public embarrassment. Conversely, relying solely on a single, rigid teaching method—such as a 30-minute lecture or silent individual seatwork—inevitably leaves learners behind. What initially appeared on the surface as Learner A’s “restlessness,” Learner C’s “distracted doodling,” or Learner D’s “lack of motivation” was actually a clear signal that the task design did not match their readiness or dominant learning style.

This experience profoundly reshaped how I approach lesson planning and classroom instruction. I realized that fairness in teaching does not mean giving every student the exact same activity; it means giving every student an accessible entry point where they can succeed. Moving forward as an educator, I am committed to designing varied, differentiated, and empathetic learning experiences—ensuring that the energetic, the quiet, the artistic, and the struggling learner all feel seen, capable, and valued inside my classroom.

Interview Guide with the Principal

A. Personal and Professional Background

The Principal’s steady rise from Teacher I in 1994 (after shifting from Civil Engineering due to financial constraints) to Principal IV, backed by a Doctorate in Educational Management, reflects organic, ground-level mastery grounded in steward, people’s, and behavioral leadership. Alongside shared decision-making, their strict time boundaries—finishing tasks at school, avoiding work messages after 7:00 PM, and protecting weekends for family—model sustainable respect for staff well-being. Listening to this journey reminded me that great educators are shaped by resilience and purpose rather than effortless paths, teaching me that true leadership means serving with both heart and healthy boundaries.

B. School Leadership and Management

Overseeing nearly 2,000 students and over 100 personnel requires clear delegation paired with emotional intelligence. The Principal practices democratic, procedural leadership—empowering Department Heads and using a “listen, think, act” approach that diffuses faculty tension by “parking” heated issues until Monday and meets student misbehavior with empathy rather than anger. What struck me most is their quiet strength in choosing composure, daily visibility, and open-door collaboration with parents and the community, showing me that trust and patience are far more powerful in guiding people than reacting out of frustration.

C. Instructional Leadership and Student Learning

The Principal’s instructional leadership is distinctly data-driven and equity-minded, scrutinizing grade summaries, LIS reports, MPS ratings, and classroom walkthroughs to ensure neither struggling nor advanced learners are neglected. By pairing creative solutions—like feeding-assisted remediation and a proposed 90-day reading and numeracy clinic—with research-backed post-tests, they demand measurable learning impact despite barriers like Saturday absences or limited home support. Hearing how deeply they examine student data and refuse to give up—even celebrating saving a small percentage of struggling learners—reminded me that behind every test score is a child who deserves a teacher willing to keep trying.

D. Closing Question (Advice for Aspiring Teachers)

In closing, the Principal emphasizes visibility, student-centeredness, and lifelong adaptability, challenging the stereotype of the “nagging” lecturer by insisting that true teaching effectiveness is measured by how actively students think critically, ask questions, and respond to a lesson. Framing leadership like an “eagle” that maintains broad perspective while staying connected to people on the ground, they define teaching as a selfless legacy built on emotional control and fairness. These parting words left a lasting mark on my heart: true teaching isn’t about how impressive I look at the front of the room, but how deeply my students are empowered to think and grow—a legacy I hope to carry into every classroom I enter.

Teacher Interview and Reflection

During our mentoring sessions at the English Nook, Ma’am Adelyn O. Damasco shared invaluable insights drawn from handling diverse Grade 8 sections—ranging from special curricular classes like G8-Jaena (SPJ), G8-Asimov (Robotics), and G8-Zara (SPTVE) to regular sections like G8-Talaghay and G8-Ulay—alongside her responsibilities as School Paper Adviser (Sunbeam) and ARAL Reading facilitator:

On Teaching Strategies: Ma’am Damasco emphasized the necessity of differentiating instruction according to each section’s unique profile and energy level, especially during afternoon-to-evening shifts that run until 7:15 PM. While special program classes like SPJ and Robotics thrive on critical inquiry, journalistic writing, and analytical challenges, technical-vocational and regular sections respond best to contextualized, hands-on, and collaborative activities. To combat late-afternoon fatigue in her 5:15 PM to 7:15 PM classes, she uses brisk interactive drills, and real-life relatable prompts to keep learners alert and engaged.

On Educational Technology: As both an English teacher and School Paper Adviser, Ma’am Damasco views educational technology as a practical bridge between classroom lessons and real-world communication. She utilizes clear, structured visual presentations, multimedia clips, and the Speech Laboratory (particularly for her Journalism I sessions) to strengthen students’ listening, speaking, and media literacy skills. Outside class hours, official Messenger group chats and digital templates are used to coordinate journalism outputs, reading interventions, and class reminders while ensuring offline accessibility for learners without steady internet access.

On Assessment Practices: Ma’am Damasco approaches assessment as both a diagnostic tool and a platform for authentic expression. In her ARAL Reading sessions and regular English classes, she relies on targeted formative checks—oral reading assessments, quick comprehension drills, and guided feedback—to catch foundational literacy gaps early. For summative evaluations, she balances standard written tests with rubric-guided performance tasks such as news/feature writing, collaborative presentations, and oral recitations so every learner can demonstrate mastery meaningfully.

On Becoming a Teacher

A. Teachers’ Roles, Responsibilities and Professional Growth

Ma’am Damasco shared that a modern teacher’s role extends far beyond delivering a 40-minute lesson plan. Balancing five Grade 8 English sections alongside duties as Grade 8 – Jaena (SPJ) Class Adviser, School Paper Adviser (Sunbeam), and ARAL Reading facilitator requires wearing many hats—acting simultaneously as instructional designer, reading interventionist, campus journalism mentor, and second parent.

Observing Ma’am Damasco manage these overlapping responsibilities taught me that teaching is not a mere 8-to-5 routine; it is a vocation of stewardship. Her dedication showed me that true professionalism lies in carrying administrative and extracurricular loads without ever letting students feel rushed or neglected.

Both Ma’am Damasco and the School Principal emphasized that curriculum reforms—such as the shift to the MATATAG Curriculum and data-driven reading/numeracy interventions—require teachers to remain lifelong learners. Through Learning Action Cell (LAC) sessions, peer mentoring, graduate studies, and classroom action research, educators continuously refine their craft to meet evolving student needs.

Hearing how seasoned educators still attend seminars, conduct action research, and collaborate during LAC sessions humbled me. Graduating with a teaching degree is only the starting line; staying effective means remaining humble enough to keep learning, unlearning, and adapting throughout my career.

B. Reflective Journal

When I first walked through the green gates of Gordon Heights National High School, I carried the quiet anxiety every student teacher knows well: Will I know enough? Will my students listen to me? Do I truly have what it takes to become a teacher? Over the course of my internship, those questions slowly gave way to a deeper, steadier conviction.

Watching Ma’am Adelyn O. Damasco pour patience and energy into five distinct Grade 8 sections until 7:15 in the evening, listening to our School Principal speak of leading with humility and never giving up on struggling learners, and reading my Grade 8 students’ survey responses all pointed me toward the same truth. Becoming a teacher is not about standing at the front of the room with all the answers; it is about showing up every single day with preparation, patience, and genuine care.

I now understand that my professional identity is anchored in both competence and compassion. Like GHNHS’s I-CARE core values, I want to be an educator who is compassionate enough to understand a struggling reader’s silence, accommodating enough to adapt my lessons to every learner’s style, resilient enough to grow from every post-observation critique, and excellent enough to give my students the highest standard of teaching they deserve. I am no longer just studying to become a teacher—I am actively stepping into the calling, grateful for the mentors and learners who shaped my beginning.

Field Study 1 · FS1–6

Classroom-Based Action Research (CBAR): Initial Inquiry

Proposed CBAR Topic and Rationale (Chapter 1)

  • Proposed Action Research Title: CLUE-QUEST: ENHANCING GRADE 8 LEARNERS’ READING COMPREHENSION THROUGH CONTEXT-CLUE VOCABULARY WORKSHEETS
  • Target Macroskill: Reading Comprehension
  • Proposed Intervention: Structured, contextualized vocabulary enrichment activities (such as pre-reading vocabulary unlocking, context-clue mapping, …)

Context and Rationale

Reading comprehension is a foundational competency for secondary academic success, yet many junior high school learners experience a persistent gap between word recognition and deep text understanding. Across Asian ESL/EFL contexts—including Indonesia (Nanda & Azmy, 2020), Thailand (Chanthap & Wasanasomsithi, 2019), and Malaysia (Sidek & Rahim, 2015)— research consistently attributes weak reading comprehension to limited vocabulary mastery, low motivation, and an inability to unlock unfamiliar words using context clues. Within the Philippines, despite national literacy initiatives like Phil-IRI and Bawat Bata Bumabasa (3Bs), both the 2022 PISA results (OECD, 2023) and local studies by Tomas et al. (2021) and Panado (2023) in Olongapo City confirm that Filipino learners continue to struggle with vocabulary depth, reading interest, and text interpretation.

At Gordon Heights National High School in Olongapo City, School Year 2026–2027 baseline Phil-IRI results for the 34 assessed learners of Grade 8–Jaena revealed a critical instructional gap: while 100% achieved Independent-level word reading accuracy (97%–100%), none met the Grade 8 comprehension standard. Instead, 23 learners read two grade levels below expectations and 11 read three grade levels below, with 58.82% (20 out of 34) falling into Instructional (17) and Frustration (3) levels even on below-grade-level passages. Classroom observations confirmed that learners stalled on unfamiliar words, guessed meanings randomly instead of using context clues, and disengaged during traditional worksheet drills.

To address this lexical and comprehension gap through active, motivating problem-solving (Qiao et al., 2022), the researcher developed CLUE-QUEST (Contextualized Lexical Understanding & Engagement Quests)—a series of print-based, gamified detective worksheets designed to train learners in context-clue vocabulary unlocking alongside literal and inferential comprehension strategies. Implemented over a four-week cycle, this Classroom-Based Action Research (CBAR) evaluates the effectiveness of CLUE-QUEST in improving the vocabulary and reading comprehension performance of Grade 8–Jaena learners while providing a practical, modality-flexible classroom intervention.

Anonymized Supporting Evidence from Classroom Observations

  • Classroom Reading & Seatwork Observations: During Grade 8 English reading lessons, several students frequently paused at unfamiliar words, asked for direct Tagalog translations before attempting comprehension questions, or left open-ended inference items blank.
  • Formative Assessment Patterns: While learners performed relatively well on literal recall questions when keywords matched the text verbatim, scores dropped noticeably on items requiring vocabulary-in-context, summarizing, or interpreting figurative/ academic terms.
  • Learner Check-In Insights (Anonymized): Consistent with earlier learner observations (such as Learner D), students candidly expressed that long English passages feel intimidating primarily because “hindi po namin alam ang ibig sabihin ng mga malalalim na salita” (“we do not know the meaning of the deep/unfamiliar words”), causing them to lose track of the whole story or article.

Cooperating Teacher / Supervisor Feedback

During our pre- and post-observation discussions at the English Nook, Ma’am Adelyn O. Damasco validated this classroom concern and guided the framing of this CBAR topic:

Ma’am Damasco pointed out that vocabulary deficiency—rather than lack of interest—is the real underlying bottleneck affecting her Grade 8 students’ comprehension scores and oral participation. She advised that instead of isolating vocabulary as boring dictionary memorization, the intervention should embed brief, engaging vocabulary-building routines (such as context-clue drills, visual associations, and everyday relatable examples) right before reading tasks so students immediately apply new words in context.
